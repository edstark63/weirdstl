Thomas Stearns Eliot is the quintessential voice of twentieth-century literary despair, a man who moved to London, adopted a British accent, and wrote "The Waste Land." But the high-minded modernist angst that defined a generation of poetry was not funded by ethereal Old World wealth. It was paid for by the mass production of St. Louis clay. T.S. Eliot’s father, Henry Ware Eliot, ran the Hydraulic-Press Brick Company, churning out the dense, heavy building materials that physically constructed the very American midwest his son fled.

<!--more-->

Before St. Louis became famous for politely asking people where they went to high school, it was a city obsessed with dirt. Specifically, the rich deposits of clay sitting beneath it. 

In 1868, the Hydraulic-Press Brick Company incorporated in St. Louis with a simple, brutalist goal: take the slow, artisanal process of hand-packing clay into wooden molds and replace it with massive, steam-powered hydraulic machines. When Henry Ware Eliot joined the company as secretary in 1874, the idea of machine-pressed bricks was still viewed with suspicion by traditional builders. They assumed the bricks would be brittle. 

James Buchanan Eads, the self-taught engineer who was currently trying to span the Mississippi River with the world's first major steel bridge, decided to find out. 

Eads and his assistant, Henry Flad, put traditional hand-pressed bricks into a testing machine. They crushed at 65 tons of pressure. Then they put in one of the Hydraulic-Press Brick Company's new machine-made bricks. It held until 157 tons. Eads bought them for the bridge, and the company’s reputation was instantly cemented. 

Under Henry Eliot’s eventual leadership as President and Chairman of the Board, the Hydraulic-Press Brick Company grew into a terrifying industrial behemoth. By 1890, their St. Louis operations alone spanned over 200 acres and employed 500 workers. They opened branches across the country and became the largest manufacturer of pressed bricks in the world, producing hundreds of millions of their signature "Hy-Tex" bricks annually. 

They built the Anheuser-Busch Brew House. They built Eliot's own family home on Westminster Place. They built countless miles of the red-brick city that T.S. Eliot was born into in 1888.

St. Louis does this sometimes. It produces a towering figure of high culture, only to reveal that their foundation is profoundly, almost comically, industrial. 

While Henry Eliot was overseeing the excavation of thousands of tons of Missouri mud, his son was attending the Smith Academy in St. Louis, writing early poetry, and preparing for Harvard. The immense fortune generated by the Hydraulic-Press Brick Company paid for young Thomas to study at Oxford and the Sorbonne. It allowed him to settle in London, work briefly at a bank, and eventually take a position at the publishing house Faber and Faber.

It is difficult to imagine a starker contrast between father and son. One spent his life standardizing the physical building blocks of American urban expansion; the other spent his life deconstructing the psychological architecture of the modern soul. Henry Eliot dealt in compression and density. T.S. Eliot dealt in fragmentation and hollow men.

But the poet never entirely escaped the brickyard. 

When T.S. Eliot won the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1948, recognized globally as the paramount voice of modern poetry, he still held stock in the Hydraulic-Press Brick Company back in St. Louis. The company continued producing bricks until 1972, long after the poet had died, leaving behind a city still largely encased in the very product that bought his ticket out.
